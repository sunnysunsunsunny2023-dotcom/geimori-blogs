Coffee grinder guide
Conical and flat burr grinders break coffee beans in different ways, but **neither burr shape automatically makes better coffee**. What you taste depends on the complete burr design, the range of particles it produces, the grind setting, and your brewing recipe—not simply whether the burrs are conical or flat.
**Quick answer:** Conical burr grinders are often associated with a rounder, more integrated cup, while some flat burr grinders are chosen for clearer separation between flavor notes. These are useful *starting tendencies*, not guarantees. Both designs can produce excellent espresso and filter coffee.
This guide explains the mechanical differences, what the science can—and cannot—tell us about flavor, and how to choose based on what you actually drink.

## How do conical and flat burr grinders work?
### Conical burrs
A conical burr set places a cone-shaped inner burr inside a ring-shaped outer burr. Beans enter from above, travel through cutting zones as they move downward, and leave the burr chamber as ground coffee. The tooth pattern, cutting geometry, feed rate, and burr alignment all influence the result.
### Flat burrs
A flat burr set uses two facing, disc-shaped burrs. Coffee enters near the center and generally travels outward through the cutting surfaces before exiting. As with conical burrs, the individual cutting teeth and the rest of the grinder matter more than the shape label alone.
**The distinction to remember:** *Burr shape describes the layout; burr design and grinder engineering determine how that layout performs.* Neither geometry is limited to espresso or pour-over.
## Do flat burrs produce a more uniform grind?
Not automatically. Every grinder produces a distribution of particle sizes, including smaller particles commonly called *fines*. That distribution depends on burr tooth geometry, alignment, grind setting, bean properties, feed behavior, RPM, and wear. Two flat burr sets may produce very different results; so may two conical sets.
Why does this matter? In espresso, fines affect how water moves through a tightly packed coffee bed. A [2024 study published in Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-55831-x) found that increasing the proportion of fines could reduce coffee-bed permeability, slow flow, and increase extraction time under the conditions tested.
The study **did not directly compare flat and conical burr geometries**, nor did it test Geimori grinders. It is evidence that particle-size distribution matters—not proof that one burr shape is universally more uniform or flavorful.

## Which burr type is better for espresso, pour-over or milk drinks?
### Straight espresso
Espresso magnifies differences in grind adjustment, puck resistance, and extraction. If you usually enjoy syrupy, chocolate-led shots with a rounded texture, a conical-burr grinder is a reasonable starting point. If you seek identifiable fruit, florals, and note separation, a flat-burr grinder designed with those goals in mind may appeal to you.
Neither is automatically easier to dial in. Small, repeatable grind adjustments, a consistent dose, and a reliable brewing recipe are essential with either burr type. For a practical next step, read our [guide to espresso grind size and shot flow](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/coffee-facts/how-grind-size-affects-espresso).
### Pour-over and other filter brewing
Flat burrs are popular with people who enjoy exploring high-clarity filter coffee, but conical burrs can also produce excellent pour-over. Pay attention to filter-range adjustment, repeatability, the burr set's intended use, and how the coffee behaves during drawdown. Water, agitation, and brew ratio can change the cup just as much as a small grinder adjustment.
### Cappuccinos and lattes
In milk drinks, espresso strength, roast choice, and the amount of milk often matter more than subtle differences in flavor separation. A rounded espresso profile can make a satisfying classic cappuccino, while a clearly defined light-roast espresso may work well in a smaller milk drink. Choose based on your preferred drink—not on a rule that one burr shape “belongs” with milk.
## Does roast level change which burr you should choose?
**Light roast:** If you enjoy floral aromas, bright acidity, and distinct origin notes, you may want to explore a clarity-oriented burr set. Light roasts can be harder to extract fully, however, so a sharp or hollow shot is not necessarily a sign of desirable clarity.
**Medium roast:** This is an overlap zone. Both conical and flat burr grinders can offer satisfying sweetness, body, and balance. Your drink format and preferred workflow may be more important than burr shape.
**Dark roast:** If you enjoy traditional espresso with a rich, integrated presentation, a conical-burr platform may be a natural first look. That is a preference, not a restriction: flat burrs can also make excellent dark-roast espresso. Watch extraction carefully, since darker coffees can become bitter when pushed too far.
## Beyond burr shape: RPM, heat, retention and static
**RPM and heat.** Conical does not automatically mean low-speed, and flat does not automatically mean high-speed. Motors, gearing, burr size, and intended throughput determine the operating range. Nor is every slow grinder cooler or every fast grinder hotter; grinding duration and the complete design also affect temperature. See our [guide to grinder RPM and grind quality](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/buying-guide/how-rpm-can-influence-grind-quality-flow-and-flavor).
**Retention.** Grounds may remain in the burr chamber or exit chute of either type. Chute design, bellows, cleaning access, and how you dose matter more than the general burr category. Learn more about [grind retention](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/coffee-facts/understanding-coffee-grind-retention-why-it-matters-for-home-brewers).
**Static.** Static can cause grounds to cling to surfaces or scatter as they exit the grinder. It is affected by coffee, environment, grinding conditions, and any anti-static system—not determined by whether the burrs are flat or conical. Explore our guide to [RDT, ionizers, and plasma anti-static technology](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/coffee-facts/how-plasma-anti-static-technology-transforms-your-coffee-grinding-experience).

## How to choose: Three questions to ask yourself
1. **What do I drink most days?** Start with your regular espresso, milk drinks, or pour-over—not the brew method you try twice a year.
1. **Do I prefer a rounded, integrated cup or more obvious separation between notes?** These are two valid preferences, not a ranking of quality.
1. **Do I want to adjust several variables or keep the routine simple?** Variable RPM and alternative burr sets offer room to experiment; fixed speed can make everyday brewing more straightforward.
If you prioritize a familiar espresso-focused workflow and fewer decisions, consider starting with the GU63. If you want to explore flat-burr options, separate flavor notes, and speed adjustment, consider the GU64 Gen2. If you fall somewhere in between, compare the complete grinder workflow rather than the burr label alone.

## Frequently asked questions
### Are flat burrs always better for espresso clarity?
No. Some flat burr sets prioritize flavor separation, but “flat” describes geometry rather than a guaranteed cup profile. Coffee selection, grind distribution, extraction, and recipe matter too.
### Do conical burrs always make more body?
No. Certain conical burr grinders are known for a round, integrated espresso style, but flat burrs can also deliver substantial body. The coffee, recipe, and specific burr design all contribute.
### Do flat burrs always produce fewer fines?
No. Fines depend on the actual cutting geometry, burr alignment, grind setting, beans, and operating conditions. Shape alone is not a reliable measure of the particle-size distribution.
### Are flat burr grinders better for pour-over?
Not inherently. A flat burr set designed for filter clarity may suit that priority, but a conical burr grinder with a suitable filter range can also make clean, balanced pour-over.
### Are conical grinders quieter or cooler than flat grinders?
Not necessarily. Noise and heat are affected by the motor, housing, RPM, load, grinding time, and grinder construction. The burr shape alone cannot predict either one.
### Can the same grinder handle both espresso and filter coffee?
Yes, provided its adjustment range and burr design support both. Consider how easily you can switch between settings, how repeatable those settings are, and whether you prefer a more specialized burr set for your main method.
## The bottom line
**Choose the grinder, not just the burr shape.** Conical and flat burrs offer different design possibilities, but there is no universal flavor winner. Start with your preferred cup style, then compare burr sets, adjustment, RPM, retention, static control, and the daily workflow you will actually enjoy.

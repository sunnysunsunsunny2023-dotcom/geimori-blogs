What actually matters inside a flat-burr grinder
## The burrs aren't the whole story.
Both grinders have 64mm flat burrs. One lays them flat, the other stands them upright. The more useful question: what actually changes in the cup?
If you've spent any time in grinder threads, you'll have seen the same debate: DF64, Zerno, GU64, different burr layouts, different price tags, and everyone chasing a little more clarity. Here's the catch: **the direction the burrs face isn't a flavor profile.** But what happens to the beans on their way into those burrs can matter.
**The short answer:** There is good experimental evidence that *how fast beans enter a grinder* changes the grind distribution. There isn't good controlled evidence that vertical burrs simply taste better than horizontal burrs. Not the same thing.
## The question people are actually asking
In a recent [r/pourover discussion about the DF54 and Fellow Opus 2](https://www.reddit.com/r/pourover/comments/1x35130/df54_vs_fellow_opus_2_in_2026/), a C40 owner wanted the convenience of an electric grinder without giving up the juicy, high-clarity cups they already liked. They also wanted to try flat burrs and maybe make espresso now and then. Eventually they leaned toward saving up for a DF64.
That sounds like the real decision to us. If your C40 already makes a V60 you love, going electric needs to offer something you'll notice. Maybe it's easier mornings. Maybe it's a different cup profile. What you don't want is more static, more tapping, or another chute to unclog.
The Reddit thread is a collection of owners' experiences and opinions, not a controlled comparison of burr orientation.
## First, what does vertical or horizontal actually mean?
We're talking about **flat-burr grinders** in both cases, not flat burrs versus conical burrs.
On the DF64 Gen 2, the burr faces sit roughly parallel to the counter, one above the other. On the GU64, they stand upright and face each other front to back. That's the layout difference people are talking about.

### Horizontal burrs
Beans fall into a set of flat burrs arranged top to bottom. Simple to picture, widely used, and not inherently worse for flavor.
Geimori GU64 Gen2
### Vertical burrs
The burr faces stand upright. Bean feeding and grounds exit need to be designed around that arrangement; orientation alone doesn't tell you how the coffee will taste.
There's also an adjustment story here, separate from the flavor question: the outer dial changes the position of the moving-burr assembly. That changes the gap between the cutting surfaces.
![GU64 schematic illustration of a moving flat burr facing a fixed flat burr with exaggerated gap for explanation](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0569/8247/0796/files/everyday_grind_4-new_2.webp?v=1789453124)
Another illustration from the adjustment-system article: the fixed burr faces the moving burr, and changing the gap changes grind size. The illustrated gap is exaggerated for clarity; this diagram explains adjustment, not bean feeding or flavor.
One important detail: vertical does not automatically mean slow feeding. Horizontal doesn't mean the beans must rush through, either. You can slow-feed a DF64 by hand, which is exactly why the next study is useful.
## What happened when researchers slow-fed a DF64?
Kaffeemacher tested normal and slower bean feeding on DF64 grinders, with particle-size analysis supported by ZHAW (Zurich University of Applied Sciences). They didn't need to turn the burrs sideways to get a different result. They changed how the beans arrived.
### Same coffee, different feeding method
Brazil Apas / DF64 / share of particles smaller than 100 μm
Regular feed**33.0%**
Hand slow-feed**23.9%**
Mechanized slow-feed**26.2%**
Slow-feed, dialed finer**38.2%**
Source: [Kaffeemacher slow-feeding study](https://kaffeemacher.de/en/blogs/kaffeewissen/slow-feeding). The first three conditions used the same grinder setting; the fourth was dialed finer to bring espresso brewing behavior back toward the target. Changes in median particle size mean these are not matched-particle-size comparisons. These measurements are **not from GU64**.
The interesting number is 33% versus 23.9% fines when the DF64 was fed slowly by hand. Sounds like a win, right? Except the grounds also came out coarser at the same dial setting. When the researchers went finer to bring the espresso flow back, the fines fraction reached 38.2%. **Different particle size, different comparison.**
So the takeaway isn't "slow feeding always gives cleaner coffee." It's that **feeding behavior changes the grind, and then you have to dial in again.** The cup still gets the final vote.
## Okay, but does that taste better?
Sometimes. Kaffeemacher's sensory results were mixed in a pretty relatable way. Some coffees tasted more open or floral with slow feeding. Others lost a bit of texture or became harder to dial in. If you love bright, separated notes in a washed Ethiopian, that might be the trade-off you're after. If you're chasing a syrupy espresso, it might not be.
The science helps explain why. A [2024 peer-reviewed study on espresso fines](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-55831-x) showed that the fraction of small particles affects how easily water flows through the puck. Another [study of filter and French press coffee](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00217-023-04210-3) found that particle distribution affects extracted compounds, but did not establish that the narrowest distribution always produces the preferred taste.
What you'll actually notice, if the grind distribution changes:
- **Pour-over:** drawdown and clarity may change. You might hear "cleaner cup," but check what happened to the body and sweetness before calling it better.
- **Espresso:** shot time can shift even if you never touch the dial. You'll probably have to dial in again.
- **Daily use:** feeding consistency, popcorning, static and chute design can be as annoying - or as helpful - as whatever happens in the cup.
## Auger, pre-breaker, burr carrier: let's not mix these up
There's a lot going on inside a grinder. An **auger** helps move beans along the feed path. A **pre-breaker** can crack beans before the main cutting stage. A **burr carrier** holds a burr in place. One component can serve more than one purpose, but a ridged shaft in an exploded illustration is not enough to tell us which job it does on the production machine.
Our older [pre-breaker explainer](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/coffee-facts/what-is-a-prebreaker-in-a-coffee-grinder-and-why-does-it-matter) discusses an earlier component concept. That doesn't verify the geometry of the revised Gen2 feeding system. To make that connection we'd need an identified production-part photo or engineering drawing.
Why do people care about an auger at all? Because it may change how steadily beans reach the burrs. Robert McKeon Aloe's [fast-vs-slow auger experiment on a Zerno](https://rmckeon.medium.com/fast-vs-slow-auger-for-coffee-grinding-1dbc8e454e59) looked at 19 paired espresso shots across 15 roasts. The author preferred slow-feed results in his tasting, even though average extraction measurements were similar. Interesting? Definitely. A large, blinded taste trial? No.
But an auger doesn't automatically mean slow feeding, and changing motor RPM isn't the same thing as adjusting feed rate independently. That's why we would want GU64-specific particle data before making a flavor claim.

## How we'd compare a DF64 and GU64 without fooling ourselves
If we were testing them side by side, we'd use the same beans and water, warm up both grinders, repeat each shot or brew, and **dial in each machine for its best reasonable recipe**. Matching an arbitrary dial mark isn't a real taste comparison.
1. Try the same washed light roast and a medium roast, so the test isn't built around just one style of coffee.
1. Record dose, output, brew time, TDS if available, and whether the chute needed tapping or cleaning.
1. Compare perceived clarity, sweetness and body blind where possible; also note which workflow you actually want to repeat every morning.
1. If you want to isolate orientation itself, you'll need more than two complete machines: match the burr geometry, alignment, rotational speed and feeding conditions.
That's the difference between testing *which grinder you prefer* and claiming you've discovered a universal vertical-burr advantage.
## Quick answers
### Do vertical flat burrs taste better than horizontal ones?
We don't have controlled research to support that blanket claim. Burr geometry, feeding, particle distribution and dialing in matter more than orientation by itself.
### Does slow feeding make fewer fines?
It did in the cited DF64 experiment at the same grinder setting, but the grind also came out coarser. When dialed finer for espresso, that advantage did not hold in the same way.
### Is that ridged shaft in the GU64 diagram the Gen2 feeding auger?
The diagram shows the shaft, but the original article labels only the fixed burr, moving burr, plate/cam and outer dial. We can't identify the ridged section as the production Gen2 auger from this illustration alone.
### Would I notice a difference coming from a C40?
Maybe, but don't assume electric or flat burrs equal better flavor. Think about the cup profile you already like, whether you want espresso capability, and whether the everyday workflow is worth changing.
## So, does vertical vs. horizontal matter in the cup?
It can matter indirectly. The layout affects what kind of feeding and exit path a grinder can use, and feeding can change the grind distribution. But we haven't seen good evidence that simply turning the burrs upright makes a better-tasting cup.
If you're already pulling juicy, clean cups from a hand grinder, that's a good problem to have. Test a new grinder for the *cup and workflow* it delivers, not for the story on the box. You may discover a flavor you like more. You may just discover that your current setup is pretty great.
